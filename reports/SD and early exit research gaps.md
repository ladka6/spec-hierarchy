# Skip the drafter race, study drift

The most defensible openings for a small team with 1-4 A100s and 8B models are no longer in drafter architecture or tree construction. Those areas saturated in 2026: at least seven papers in five months add intra-block dependency to DFlash-style drafters, and four more compete on verify-length control. The top-ranked gaps are about how reliable speculation stays after deployment. **Rank 1 is speculation under target post-training drift**: measuring, predicting and cheaply recovering drafter acceptance across a sequence of target versions. The closest work (EDA, Draft-OPD, SpecRoll, ReSpec) covers large domain fine-tunes, AR or EAGLE heads, or RL rollouts, and none of it covers the small-drift LoRA regime where your 10-35% loss and roughly 1 GPU-hour recovery sit. **Rank 2 is a bf16 losslessness audit across SD families** with batch-invariant kernels as a control. The only close study covers one 1.7B model (Orthrus), and the gap is time-sensitive. **Rank 3 is preserving early exits through post-training** for self-speculative models: a September 2026 diagnosis paper asks for "early-exit-aware tuning", and search found no method paper. **Rank 4** turns your 0.85-0.9x HiSpec-vs-DDTree result into a crossover map, testing partial-depth verification only where verification cost actually grows (batch 16 or more, long context). Early exit on its own is a poor bet for dense 8B models: oracle exits skip at most 7.26% of layers under a 5% accuracy loss, and naive retrofits decode slower than the base model. Several novelty checks are still unverified, most importantly the unread papers 2607.26627 and 2512.21911 and phrase searches for "draftability" and "early-exit-aware fine-tuning".

## Nine 2026 clusters are already saturated

The drafter side of SD consolidated around DFlash, a 5-layer block-diffusion drafter conditioned on target features. It reaches **4.86x at T=0 and 4.03x at T=1 on Qwen3-8B** ([DFlash](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06036v1)). Most 2026 drafter work is an increment on top of it, and the increments are shrinking. Dependency-modeling papers report +9% to +18% acceptance over DFlash chains: Domino +16.6% ([Domino](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29707v1)), DSpark +16.3-18.4% ([DSpark](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05147v1)), TreeFlash +12% block efficiency ([TreeFlash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03819)), DBLAST >12% at T=1.5 ([DBLAST](https://pith.science/paper/2608.05448)). None of them reports gains over DDTree as the baseline. The serving layer is just as busy. DScale, posted 29 Sep 2026, beats DSpark by 22-38% and Domino by 24-32% on A100-40GB at concurrency 8-32 ([DScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37532)), so these methods now compete on systems margins against each other.

The early-exit side has the same problem. KV-cache repair for skipped layers and batching with heterogeneous depth each have several 2025-2026 systems answers: River-LLM, ADEPT, DREX, vSkipper and Continuous Depth Batching. What they share is sobering. vSkipper found that FlexiDepth's standard generation loop decodes **14.6-21.0% slower than the base model** despite skipping 8 of 32 layers. Its own serving gains on Llama-3-8B appear only at high load, above about 200k resident tokens ([vSkipper](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.37062)). Training-free layer-skip self-speculation has plateaued at about 1.3-1.7x. ConfLayers tops out at 1.4x ([ConfLayers](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14612)).

The distribution-preserving verification rule is also close to solved. Optimal multi-draft acceptance can be computed in O(|V| log |V|), and existing verifiers sit only **1-3% below that optimum** ([Towards Optimal Multi-Draft SD](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18779)). HSD's lossless hierarchical resampling adds about 6% on average and 12.4% on top of EAGLE-3 ([HSD](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05724v2)). Any new acceptance-rule paper therefore starts with little headroom.

## Your bounded results match the literature's blind spots

Every negative result you have lines up with something the literature leaves unmeasured. That makes the results useful for positioning, but none of them is a paper on its own.

**The 0.85-0.9x HiSpec result has a clean mechanism.** HiSpec reports 1.70x over vanilla and 2.08x at best, on 4x H100. It compared only against LayerSkip, AdaDecode and Lookahead, never against EAGLE-3 or DFlash ([HiSpec](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01336v1)). ECHO, SEED and READER also lack a strong external-drafter baseline ([ECHO](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17241), [SEED](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36590)). Your batch-1 verification cost is flat up to about 128 tokens, so an intermediate verifier can only shrink a tree whose verification is already free, or defer the full pass and risk rollback. Either way the lower-layer pass is overhead. Qwen3-8B also makes a poor intermediate verifier. Its Early-Exit Adaptability Score is 0.51, and even oracle exits in modern base models skip only **0-7.26% of layers under a 5% accuracy loss** ([Diminishing Returns of EE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.23701)). Your logit-lens measurement agrees: Qwen3-8B's half-depth prediction disagrees with the final layer 96% of the time, against 13% for LayerSkip-Llama3-8B. The open question is where the crossover lies, since cost only grows with batch size. SpecDecode-Bench shows verification taking 42-95% of execution time, and EAGLE on Llama-3-70B falling from 1.96x at batch 1 to 1.21x at batch 128 ([SD: Performance or Illusion?](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11580)).

**Stale-draft repair and async pipelines lost to target-feature reuse.** DFlow reuses the target's hidden states at rejected positions and gains 10-13% acceptance over DFlash for Qwen3 1.7B-8B ([DFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.06498)). Your finding that a stale continuation is worth 0.28 of a fresh draft is consistent with the field moving from reusing tokens to reusing features. The obvious variant of this direction is taken.

**Your LoRA drift result sits in an unmeasured regime.** EDA shows full domain fine-tuning (Qwen2.5-Math/Coder/Meditron) collapsing acceptance length from about 4.7 to 1.2. It recovers with shared frozen experts plus private experts covering about 27.5% of parameters ([EDA](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09527v1)). A vLLM RFC reports that LoRA adapters on one DFlash drafter reach within about 2% of per-domain drafters ([vLLM #52038](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/52038)). ReSpec shows EAGLE-3 acceptance declining within 100 RL steps ([ReSpec](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26475)). OnlineSPEC treats drafter updates as online learning but assumes a fixed target ([OnlineSPEC](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12617)). Your setup differs from all of these on four points: LoRA-level drift, a 10-35% loss rather than collapse, a single joint drafter with zero per-target parameters, and DDTree trees. The Scaling Laws for SD paper names the effect of RLHF on draft accuracy as an open question ([Scylla](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07858)).

**The bf16 divergence is a field-wide property.** Orthrus matches greedy AR decoding on only 43-45% of 1,190 prompts in BF16 and on 100% in FP32. Divergence tracks response perplexity, with no downstream accuracy loss ([How Lossless Is Lossless SD?](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15504v1)). A separate study finds BF16 and FP16 greedy outputs differ on **49-100% of prompts** across six models ([Greedy Decoding Is Not Precision-Invariant](https://awesomepapers.io/llm-papers/papers/2609.26621)). Batch SD Done Right attributes its residual 5% non-equivalence to floating-point nondeterminism ([2510.22876](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22876v3)). Your rate of 15-19 divergent outputs out of 40 (38-48%), the same for every method including DDTree, falls in this band.

**Dependency heads and block trees gain little over DDTree.** At +5-7% over DDTree, your number is plausibly what remains after DDTree's tree absorbs most of the +9-18% that others report over DFlash chains. That reading is an inference: no paper found reports dependency heads against DDTree.

Ranks 1, 3 and 8 together make one coherent thesis, "speculation under post-training". It covers both external drafters and self-drafts, fits a continual-learning research identity, and reuses every piece of your existing infrastructure. The theory gap of predicting acceptance from offline drafter and target statistics folds into rank 1 as its predictive component. The closest per-token theory, KL-threshold certificates for acceptance, stops short of a trajectory-level predictor ([When Is a Draft Accepted?](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30265v1)).

Rank 7 is the cleanest gap in the whole survey: 2609.22156 names the online policy at batch above 1 as future work, and it ran its entire study on one A100 80GB ([Limits of Speculation in MoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22156)). It ranks low only because it has nothing to do with your DFlash and early-exit work. The same limitation-section evidence shows that hybrid linear-attention targets remain unsolved: sequential hybrids reach α=0.038 and wall-clock speedups stay below 1.0x ([2605.01106](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01106v1)). The engineering cost there is too high for this team.

## First experiments and kill criteria for the top five

### Rank 1: drift-aware drafter maintenance

Build about eight Qwen3-8B target variants that span drift size: LoRA at several ranks and step counts, one full domain SFT, and one DPO checkpoint. For each variant, measure DDTree acceptance length and tokens/s with the frozen joint DFlash drafter. Compute three cheap drift statistics: token-level KL between new and old targets on drafter-context samples, top-1 agreement on held-out prompts, and LoRA weight norm. Fit the speed loss against each statistic. The second step is sequential. Update the joint drafter across versions v1 to v4, comparing replay, EDA-style private adapters and retraining from scratch, and measure forgetting on earlier versions. Position the work explicitly against EDA's large-drift regime and SpecRoll's EAGLE-only, math-only setup. **Kill criterion:** stop the predictive angle if no single cheap statistic ranks speed loss across variants with Spearman ρ of at least 0.7. Stop the maintenance angle if recovery stays at about 1 GPU-hour regardless of drift size, including the full SFT and DPO variants. In that case the result shrinks to a workshop note ("LoRA drift is cheap to fix").

### Rank 2: numerical losslessness of speculative decoding

Scale the 40-prompt measurement to 500 or more prompts across math, code and chat. Run four conditions. (a) AR against AR while varying batch size and sequence shape, which gives the noise floor. (b) Each SD method against AR with default kernels: DDTree, EAGLE-3, a draft model and LayerSkip self-speculation. (c) The same under vLLM batch-invariant mode, which supports CC 8.0 and therefore A100 ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/batch_invariance/)). (d) An FP32 LM head. Log the target's top-1/top-2 margin at each first divergence, and check whether divergence changes task accuracy. As a follow-up method, recompute in FP32 inside verification only when the margin is low, applying the trigger from 2609.26621 to SD. **Kill criterion:** the audit shrinks to a short note if SD-vs-AR divergence equals the AR-vs-AR noise floor and batch-invariant kernels drive it to near zero for every method, since that only confirms the known batch-invariance cause ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). Drop it outright if a multi-method follow-up to 2609.15504 appears first. Also check before starting whether vLLM's batch-invariant mode runs with speculative decoding at all, because its documentation does not say.

### Rank 3: exit-preserving post-training

First fix a confound. Your code and chat LoRAs also collapse on GSM8K, so part of their larger self-speculation loss may be task drift rather than exit erosion. Use LoRAs that preserve GSM8K accuracy. Then train LayerSkip-Llama3-8B with (a) plain LoRA, (b) LoRA plus an early-exit loss at the draft layer, and (c) LoRA plus layer dropout. Measure half-depth agreement, self-speculation acceptance and task accuracy. An optional extension measures how many LoRA tokens with an early-exit loss move Qwen3-8B's 96% half-depth disagreement toward LayerSkip's 13%. **Kill criterion:** stop if (b) or (c) recovers less than half of the roughly 10% self-speculation loss at equal task accuracy. Keep impact expectations modest either way. Self-drafting at 8B still trails DFlash (HiSpec's 2.08x against DFlash's 4.86x), so this work matters for settings without an external drafter, not as a speed record.

### Rank 4: partial-depth verification where cost scales

Profile Qwen3-8B verification time against total verified tokens (batch size × tree nodes) on an A100 to find where cost stops being flat. Your measurement puts the knee above 128 tokens at batch 1. Above the knee, test in-pass tree pruning. A TIDE-style or ECHO-style trained head at L/4 drops low-probability nodes, and the upper layers run only on the survivors. The output stays exact because acceptance still uses final logits. Separately, test a two-level long-context design: SpecPV-style partial-KV verification between the DFlash drafter and full-KV verification ([SpecPV](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02337v1)). **Kill criterion:** compute the upper bound on savings as (upper-layer fraction) × (pruned-node fraction) × (marginal cost slope) at batch 32 with a DDTree budget. Stop if that bound, minus the head's cost and the lost acceptance length, projects to less than 5% net gain. Read 2512.21911 before writing any code.

### Rank 5: DDTree under sampling

Implement exact multi-candidate rejection (recursive rejection sampling) for DDTree trees. Measure acceptance length at T of 0.6 and 1.0 against DFlash chains and DDTree with the naive rule, then test tree scoring that accounts for sampling instead of top-k marginals. DFlash itself loses about 17% of its speedup going from T=0 to T=1 ([DFlash](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06036v1)), which shows room exists. **Kill criterion:** since verification rules already sit within 1-3% of optimal, all gains must come from tree construction. Stop if sampling-aware trees add less than 5% acceptance length over DDTree at T=1.

## Conclusion

Your results add up to a regime statement: at batch 1 on an A100 with an 8B target, verification is effectively free up to about 128 tokens. No method that saves verification work can win there, and gains have to come from draft quality, which is exactly where the 2026 papers are crowding. The productive move is to stop competing on speed at batch 1 and to study the properties that decide whether speculation survives deployment: drift after target updates, exits eroding under post-training, and bf16 non-equivalence. These questions are cheap at 8B and underexplored. They also map onto a continual-learning identity better than another drafter variant would.

Two timing points follow. The bf16 audit is the fastest to publish and the most likely to be scooped, since 2609.15504 appeared this month and a multi-method follow-up is the obvious next step for its authors. It should start first as a short standalone paper. The drift study is the thesis backbone. Its value depends on finding a drift regime where recovery cost actually scales with drift, so the first experiment should include at least one full-SFT or DPO target alongside the LoRAs.
