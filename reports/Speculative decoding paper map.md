# Mapping speculative decoding papers and their weak spots

As of October 2026, the speculative decoding (SD) literature has a few clear anchors. The lossless draft-then-verify rule comes from Leviathan et al. and Chen et al. **EAGLE-3** is the reference autoregressive drafter, with 4-6x at batch 1 and T=0. **DFlash** (ICML 2026) is the block-diffusion drafter that most 2026 papers use as backbone or baseline. **DDTree** turns DFlash's marginals into draft trees. On the lossless verification side, **Block Verification, Traversal Verification, UniVer and HSD** each add only 2-12% acceptance length, and only at T>0. The lossy line ranges from learned judges (Judge Decoding, AutoJudge) through training-free semantic acceptance (FLy) to step-level speculation for reasoning models (RSD, SpecReason, Lookahead Reasoning). The weaknesses are remarkably consistent across all five categories. Headline numbers are almost always batch 1, usually T=0, often in HF Transformers rather than vLLM or SGLang, and measured against baselines each paper re-runs itself. Where papers do report batch scaling, the gains shrink: EAGLE-3 falls to 1.01x at batch 56 in vLLM, and DFlash falls from 5.1x to 2.8x at concurrency 32 in SGLang. Drafters are also tied to one target, so every fine-tune of the target requires retraining the drafter. This map covers about 100 papers with method, headline result, code, stated and inferred weaknesses, and a verification tag. Many numbers in it were not re-checked against the PDFs this session (arXiv was rate-limited); those numbers are marked **(unv.)** and must be checked before the downside analysis quotes them.

## How to read the tables

Every row gives the arXiv ID (linked), venue and first-posting date, a one-line method, the headline result with its setting, code, limitations, and a verification tag. In the limitations column, "S:" marks what the authors state themselves and "I:" marks inferred weaknesses, which are the research team's reading and not author claims. Venues marked "?" are unconfirmed.

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **F** | Full paper or arXiv HTML read this session; numbers taken from it |
| **X** | arXiv HTML read through a summarizing extractor; numbers plausible but should be checked against the PDF |
| **A** | Abstract, project page, README, or secondary summary only; setting details may be incomplete |
| **P** | Taken from the team's earlier notes (Sep 2026), not re-read this session |
| **K** | ID/title confirmed, but method and numbers recalled from background knowledge; **numbers unverified** |

Papers that fit several categories appear once, with a cross-reference elsewhere: Medusa (cat. 1, typical acceptance in cat. 4), EAGLE-2 (cat. 1, tree role in cat. 5), MagicDec (cat. 5), HSD (cat. 5).

## 1. Autoregressive drafters: EAGLE-3 is the bar, and batch scaling is its weak point

The line runs in three stages. Separate small drafters came first, along with work aligning them to the target. Draft heads that reuse target hidden states came next (Medusa, Hydra, EAGLE, EAGLE-3). Since 2025, the work has been training objectives that optimize acceptance directly and drafters that adapt after deployment. The most important single number in this category comes from the EAGLE-3 paper's own vLLM table: the speedup is **1.75x at batch 2 and 1.01x at batch 56** (chain length 2, no tree) ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v1)).

### Shared weaknesses in AR drafters

Almost every paper reports batch-1 latency, and the one that measures batching (EAGLE-3) shows the gain collapsing. **Drafters are target-specific**: each new target or fine-tune means retraining, and EDA and OnlineSPEC only partly address this. Training objectives keep rediscovering that KL is a proxy for acceptance (TVD++, AdaSPEC, LK, VAT, GTO). Their gains over EAGLE-3 are **5-25% at T=0 on 4-8B Qwen3/LLaMA-3.1 targets on a single A100**. Several papers mask or drop hard tokens (AdaSPEC, GRIFFIN), which can raise average acceptance while hiding tail behavior. Online adaptation papers compare against weak baselines and never against a strong offline EAGLE-3.

## 2. Self-speculation and training-free drafting tops out near 2x outside special regimes

Without a separately trained drafter, lossless speedups on general chat sit at **about 1.3-2.2x at batch 1**. They exceed that only in three regimes: long context (quantized or sparse-KV self-drafts), repetitive or agentic workloads (suffix and n-gram drafting), or models pretrained with multi-token prediction (MTP). Training-free layer skipping keeps 40-60% of target depth per draft token, which caps it well below a one-layer external drafter.

### Shared weaknesses in self-speculation and training-free drafting

Batch 1 is again near-universal, with MagicDec the only exception. **Speedup metrics are not comparable across papers**: the MTP papers report batch-42 throughput, acceptance-based tokens per step, and batch-1 SGLang wall-clock. The model-free methods are evaluated mostly on Vicuna or CodeLlama with Spec-Bench, which SAM-Decoding's own authors say misrepresents real retrieval usage. Many of the methods described as "self" or "training-free" do train something (Kangaroo adapters, LayerSkip, CLLMs, all MTP work) or change the losslessness reference (QSpec, SpecEE, CLLMs). Few compare against a trained external drafter, and HiSpec loses to DDTree in the team's own runs.

## 3. Parallel and diffusion drafters: about 20 DFlash follow-ups in five months

Parallel drafting runs from Blockwise Parallel Decoding (2018) through PARD and diffusion-LM drafters (2025) to **DFlash** (Feb 2026, ICML 2026), which became the default backbone. Since May 2026 the follow-ups fall into four groups: restoring dependency between tokens inside a block, building trees over block marginals, serving and scheduling, and training objectives. Nearly all use Qwen3-4B/8B targets with headline numbers at batch 1, T=0, in HF Transformers. Rows tagged X were read through a summarizing extractor and need a PDF check.

### Shared weaknesses in parallel drafters

**Baselines disagree.** DFlash on Qwen3-8B is reported at 4.16x (D²SD, H200), 4.61x (PARD-2, A100 vLLM) and 4.86x (DFlash paper), and DARTree's DDTree re-run is well below DDTree's own numbers. **Few papers use DDTree as a baseline**, and none compares trees against each other at batch above 1. Fixed block size and test-set budget selection are common. Serving claims come either from internal production (DSpark) or from a single concurrency point (AdaFlash). T=1 results are often in appendices, and the T=1 DFlash baseline drafts greedily. "Lossless" is asserted but never audited in bf16. The team's own runs found 15-19 of 40 outputs diverging from plain greedy for every SD method, which is consistent with the 43-45% greedy match rate in BF16 reported by [How Lossless Is Lossless SD?](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15504v1). No independent reproduction of any 2026 paper exists outside the team's own DFlash/DDTree runs.

## 4. Lossy and relaxed SD gives up the distribution guarantee and rarely measures the cost

Lossy methods accept draft tokens or whole steps that the target would not have produced exactly. Token-level methods use thresholds, learned judges or semantic signals. Cascades target a deferral-defined mixture. Step-level methods accept whole reasoning steps by reward, score or semantic equivalence. Only Mentored decoding and DIVERSED state anything formal about the output distribution. The 2026 analysis paper "Revisiting Lossy Verification" splits the field into truncation-based and collaborative families and names failure modes for each ([HF page](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.26627)).

### Shared weaknesses in lossy SD

Quality is measured only by benchmark accuracy (GSM8K, HumanEval, MT-Bench). **No paper reports distribution-level metrics** such as diversity, calibration or safety refusals, although Judge Decoding's authors flag safety themselves. ## 5. Lossless verification is nearly saturated, and serving work explains why speedups erode

Verification rules moved through four stages: token-wise rejection, multi-draft optimal transport, sequence-level joint verification, and a unified conditional-OT view. **Each step adds less**: SpecTr ~37% over SD, Block ~8%, Traversal ~4%, UniVer ~7% over recursive rejection, HSD ~6%. All of these gains require T>0 and vanish at greedy decoding. The earlier team report found that existing verifiers sit only 1-3% below the multi-draft optimum ([Towards Optimal Multi-Draft SD](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18779)). Tree construction moved from heuristics to DP and latency-aware trees, all single-request. Serving papers show the speedup disappearing under load.

### Shared weaknesses in verification, trees and serving

Verification papers evaluate at T≥0.6 on a single consumer or A100 GPU at batch 1. None of them measures its rule inside vLLM or SGLang continuous batching; the vLLM block-verification PR is the closest. Systems papers headline at T=0 (SSD, PEARL, Batch SD Done Right), and SSD degrades as temperature rises, so no paper tests a strong verifier and a serving system together at realistic T and batch above 1. **EAGLE-style top-K drafting gives one-hot draft probabilities**, which weakens probability-based verifiers. HSD says this outright, and the RheoSampling title (2609.21827, title only) points at the same "one-hot dilemma". Tree builders optimize single-request objectives, and verifiers and trees are rarely co-designed (Sequoia and UniVer are the exceptions). Several systems results are not iso-hardware: PEARL and SSD use an extra draft GPU.

Read these first, in this order. **EAGLE-3** sets the AR baseline and contains the batch-collapse table. **DFlash** and **DDTree** define the team's own stack and its known weak points. **HSD** is closest to the hierarchy branch and states the one-hot confound. **Batch SD Done Right** and the **Interpretable Latency Model** explain correctness and erosion under batching. **MagicDec** shows the one regime where SD survives large batch. **SWIFT** and **HiSpec** set the self-speculation ceiling, and HiSpec is the method the team already beat. **Revisiting Lossy Verification** and **Judge Decoding** cover the lossy side. **DSpark** is the strongest serving-aware parallel drafter. ## Conclusion

Across all five categories, the methods differ far more than their evaluation habits do. Drafter architectures, verification rules, lossy acceptance criteria and serving schedulers all report their best numbers in the same narrow regime: one request, greedy decoding, a 4-8B model, and often an HF backend. Most of them have not shown how they behave outside it. This means the most productive improvement angles are probably not new drafters or new acceptance rules, where gains are already down to single-digit percentages over EAGLE-3, DFlash or DDTree. They are measurements and methods that hold up when the regime changes: batch size, temperature, serving engine, target updates, and bf16 numerics.

Two specific openings follow from the map. First, no paper evaluates an optimal lossless verifier together with a tree builder and a batching-aware serving system at T between 0.6 and 1.0. The categories that would need to meet for that study were developed separately. Second, the lossy literature has no distribution-level evaluation at all. An audit of what judges and semantic acceptance change beyond benchmark accuracy would be cheap to run, and the papers' own limitation sections already ask for it.
